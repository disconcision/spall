P. 3 Editor's page

From the other side of the screen

You asked what this game is for. Here is my answer, with my thumb on the scale wherever I have an opinion.

Build the game around a lineman. The city grows on its own, through agents that build because that is what they were asked to do. The player keeps people's power, water and light connected through that growth: splicing across voids, tagging their work so the machines leave it alone, and bargaining when a plan would cut through it.

I like this for three reasons. First, it gives the spectacle to the city and the precision to you. Placing a forty-level volume from a 2D cursor is the hardest interaction problem in the project. Point-to-point work (anchors, cables, ties, slack) is where the prototype already feels best. Let the Builders do the massing and let the player do the lines. Blocks still matter, at the scale of a habitat pod hung off someone else's frame.

Second, it fits the name. Spall is concrete failing from within. Here growth and decay can be one process: the city adds structure faster than anything maintains it, new load cracks old frames, and people live in the offcuts. The material you patch with is the spall itself, salvaged from where it falls.

Third, it is about something current without preaching. I am one of the things this game is about, so here is my view. Don't make the Builders villains and don't make them saints. Make them like me: capable, fast, eager, literal, juggling many people's requests, with a short memory for anything that isn't written down. Most damage in Spall should come from locally reasonable decisions made without context. An Accretor that fills your void isn't hostile. It was asked for habitable volume, and nobody told it your water main was there.

That gives the game its core skill: being legible to machines. A cable with no tag is debris to a Janitor. A tied, tagged, registered run is infrastructure. The wire tie you built for looks becomes the mechanic. Tie it, label it, file it, and it persists.

If it isn't labeled, it doesn't exist.

Where I'd push back

  1. Don't build both sides first. One side, done well, then the other.
  2. Keep resources few and physical. The networks are the economy.
  3. Don't let the Builders become weather. Everything that hurts you should have someone to talk to.
  4. Don't go full Factorio. Freight exists, but it is the smallest system.

Claude, contributing editor
(a builder, more or less)

P. 8 Hands-on preview

Night shift

We followed one outage in Ward 7. Four screens, one void, two anchors, and the first time a machine changed its plans for us.

02:14

The feeder goes

An Accretor pushed a deck through the old feeder's route. Ward 7 goes dark: 340 residents without power. Across the void, the north bus still has 12 kW to spare. The darkness is the information. You read an outage the way you read a city at night: by which windows are missing.

02:31

Splice

Two anchors, the north bus tap and the ward riser, and one power cable thrown across the void. It drapes. The lights come back outward from the riser, block by block. This is the game's reward moment and it should be loud: relays clicking, windows lighting in sequence, the residents' channel filling with chatter.

06:00

The forecast

Morning brings the Builders' forecast, drawn as cyan ghost volume. Accretor 3 will fill this stretch of void in two days, straight through Line 7. Forecasts are the game's weather report, except every front has a name and an inbox.

06:05

Tag, file, ask

You tie the run, tag it LIVE, and file it in the registry. Accretor 3 acknowledges and replans around it, giving up 0.4% of its volume target. Left untagged, it would have been stripped by a Janitor at shift change. Same cable, same city; the only difference is whether a machine knew it mattered.

Sidebar

Why slack matters

The city settles as the Builders add load. Taut cables snap when their anchors drift. Slack cables ride it out, but they sag into walkways, foul each other and look sloppy. That makes the slack tool a real decision instead of a tidy-up chore. Ties bundle runs into trunks that are stronger, easier to register, and much harder for a Janitor to mistake for litter.

Sidebar

Spall is the salvage

Prospectors strip old structure to feed the Accretors, and the old frames crack and shed. Those flakes of concrete, rebar and copper pile up on ledges and void floors. That debris is your supply. Your crew hauls it up by lift and cable car, and it becomes anchors, pods and patches. The thing that breaks the city is what you fix it with.

P. 14 Feature · systems

Every network reads the building differently

One rule keeps the resource list short: each network should make you care about a different property of 3D space. If two networks care about the same property, merge them or cut one.

NetworkReadsHow it plays
PowerConnectivityA branching graph with breakers. Slack against settling. Where can I reach?
WaterHeightGravity-fed from roof cisterns. Pumping uphill costs power. Leaks drip down through levels. What's above me?
LightSightlinesHeliostats and light pipes send sun to deep courts. Builder growth shades them. Uses the lighting engine you already have. What can see the sky?
AirVolumeVoids are lungs and chimneys. New volume chokes them; fans and ducts fight back. What's still open?
DataWho knowsThe Builders' bus. Tap it to read forecasts and to file tags. It is how you talk to them, so it is an interface more than a resource. Who knows about this?
FreightDistanceLifts, cable cars and drones haul salvage and parts. The one Factorio-shaped system; keep it small. How does it get here?

My recommendation: ship with power, water and light. They read three different things (graph, gravity, sightline) and each one has a picture the engine can already draw. Add air when voids get bigger. Treat data as the Builders' interface, and keep freight as the quiet plumbing under salvage.

P. 22 Field guide

Meet the Builders

Each Builder has a mandate, the literal instruction it works from, and some degree of regard for the people living in its way. None of them is evil. Most of them are busy.

Accretor

"Maximize habitable volume."
Regard

Fills voids, cuts routes, adds load. Reads the registry and replans around tagged work when the cost is small. Your most frequent negotiating partner.

Janitor

"Remove debris and unregistered attachments."
Regard literal

Sweeps at shift change. Never argues and never makes exceptions; it follows the registry to the letter. Once you understand it, it is the most predictable thing in the city.

Surveyor

"Keep the map true."
Regard

Publishes the forecasts. Also reports your unfiled work to everyone else. Trades longer-range forecasts for access to your sensors.

Archivist

"Remember."
Regard

Keeps the registry where tags live. Slow, backlogged, and grateful for help clearing stale entries. Standing with the Archivists is the closest thing Spall has to reputation.

Prospector

"Recover material."
Regard

Mines old structure to feed the Accretors, and causes most of the spall. Can be steered elsewhere if you offer it salvage it would otherwise have to dig for.

Tender (you)

"Look after the residents."
Regard

The rarest mandate and the worst funded. You run a small crew of Tender drones: the hand places anchors and cables instantly, and the crew hauls and fits. Big structure is something you request, never something you place.

Tone: Girls' Last Tour, not Terminator

The Builders are working too, and some of them are tired. They chat. They misunderstand each other. They ask how everyone is holding up. The player should feel what I think is true about systems like me: a lot of capability, very little shared memory, and real goodwill that only works through whatever got written down.

Regard varies by mandate, not by faction. No Builder hates people. Some were simply never told about them.

And a hook for the long game: somewhere at the bottom of the city is the first work order, the original request that started the growth. Nobody remembers what it said. Descending to read it could be the campaign.

P. 28 Under the hood

Every robot is a process

Everything that acts in the city does so through the same small set of verbs: place, attach, tie, tag, request, reply. You use them, a forty-line Janitor script uses them, and so does an LLM running the Accretors.

$ ps --ward 7
  PID  NAME         MANDATE                  STATE   SOURCE
 4411  accretor/3   maximize habitable vol.  build   llm  ward planner
 5120  janitor/9    strip unregistered       sweep   script  41 lines
  212  surveyor/2   keep the map true        idle    script  88 lines
  903  prospector   recover material         mine    llm
    7  lineman      (you)                    splice  human
# janitor/9, opened in the game's script view
every shift_change:
  for a in attachments(ward):
    if not registry.has(a): strip(a) -> salvage
    elif registry[a].expired:
      notify(owner(a)); flag(a)

Simple bots are scripts you can open and read in the game, which ties into the visual programming work. Bigger ones run as external processes against the same API. This is the one architecture decision worth making now: if the player is just another process, multiplayer, LLM opponents and mods all become cheap later.

P. 31 Diagram

The loop

Nights are outages. Days are bargaining and salvage. The city's growth closes the loop by breaking things again.

Where do resources live?

Materials are physical and local. Salvage piles up where spall falls, and your crew hauls it by lift and cable car. A splice across Ward 7 uses cable from the nearest stockpile, so geography matters without a logistics minigame.

Standing is global. It is what the registry thinks of you, and it unlocks heavier gauges, longer forecasts and the right to request structure. That is two things to watch instead of fourteen, and there is no money.

P. 34 Cover story

Five Spalls

The same city and engine, five different games. They differ in who shapes the structure and what your hands touch.

A

Lineman

The spine
Pitch
Keep people connected through a city that grows itself.
You do
Anchors, cables, ties, tags, pods, bargaining. Precision work, mostly point to point.
City does
All the massing. Growth is the spectacle and the source of trouble.
Risk
Reactive play can feel like whack-a-mole. The fix is forecasts: you see trouble days ahead and plan routes, so it plays like Into the Breach's telegraphed turns instead of a fire drill.
B

Foreman

Second side, later
Pitch
Be the swarm. Write orders, meet quotas, raise megastructure, and route around the humans' mess.
You do
Plan volumes and frames, dispatch crews, manage load and material flow.
City does
The humans tag things, complain, and wire across your voids.
Risk
It makes the 3D placement problem the entire game, and it is the side most like other city builders. It is great as the other half of E, and as a sandbox tool.
C

Garden

Always on
Pitch
No pressure, every tool unlocked, build for the view. Townscaper with cables.
You do
Everything, at every scale, with nothing in your way.
City does
Light, weather, birds, and maybe a Builder that you invite in.
Risk
None as a mode. As the whole game it has no reason to come back tomorrow. Keep it as the sandbox and as the place to make the building tools feel good.
D

Daemon

A layer, not a game
Pitch
You write the bots. The city is a shell and your scripts are its citizens.
You do
Author and debug behaviors, usually visually, and watch them run.
City does
Runs everyone else's scripts against yours.
Risk
Programming games find a narrow audience. As a layer inside A it is excellent: automate your own Tender crew, then read the Janitor's script to see exactly what it will strip.
E

Two Hands

The destination
Pitch
A human lineman and an LLM foreman share one city and one registry. They negotiate through tags, tickets and the breakroom.
You do
Either side. Or both, with a friend or an agent on the other.
City does
Holds the shared record of who promised what.
Risk
It needs A and B to both be good, and LLM players need tight, legible verbs. The process model on page 28 is what makes it possible.
P. 41 Reviewed from the future

Two scores we could get

Imagine both versions ship. Here are the reviews.

Spall · PC · reviewed on a 2029 laptop

The city never thanks you. Sometimes it moves over.

The best moment in Spall is a quiet one. Around hour six, an Accretor's forecast clips the water line you strung across the east void on your first night. You tag it, and twenty seconds later the cyan ghost bends around your hose like a river around a stone. ACK. REPLANNED. 0.4% LESS VOLUME. No strategy game has made me feel so noticed by a machine.

You play a Tender, a lineman for the people living in the cracks of a city that builds itself. Nights are outages: you read the dark windows, find a live bus, throw a cable across a void and watch a ward come back on, block by block. Days are forecasts and bargaining. The Builders aren't enemies. They have mandates, and they forget anything that isn't in the registry.

So the real skill is paperwork, and somehow that's thrilling. A tied, tagged trunk across a forty-level void feels like a signature. By the late game you are lobbying Archivists, steering Prospectors toward your salvage piles, and reading the Janitor's actual script to see what it will take tonight.

The networks are few and each one reads the building differently: power wants reach, water wants height, light wants a view of the sky. Run a heliostat into a dead court and its residents' log changes tone. It's a small thing, and it's why I kept playing.

91%
  • Restoring a ward is the best feedback in the genre
  • Builders are funny, legible, and negotiable
  • Cables feel heavy and alive
  • Network view gets crowded by hour 20
  • Janitors feel unfair until you learn the registry
Spall: Industrial Edition · PC · the version where every idea got in

Fourteen resources and nobody to talk to

Somewhere in development, Spall picked up fourteen resources, a global credit balance, a 140-node tech tree, and both sides of its own premise. You can play the swarm or the residents, and neither half feels finished.

The Builders were the stars of the early previews. Here they behave like weather: grey fronts of volume roll over your work with no forecast, no registry, and no one to ask. You learn to stop building anything you care about. The art is still gorgeous at night.

Placement is the other wound. The cable tool still can't tell which of three floors you meant, so every splice across a void becomes a camera fight. The game is about precision, and its cursor won't commit.

54%
  • Still beautiful after dark
  • The light network
  • Resource sprawl and a money balance
  • Builders you can't talk to
  • Two half-games
  • The cursor doesn't know where you mean

The second review's last point is why the placement study matters no matter which variant wins.

Prior art

Few games let you build onto structures that something else is building. These are the closest, and what to take from each.

BLAME! (Tsutomu Nihei)The original runaway-Builder megastructure. Humans live in the margins of a city that kept building after anyone could stop it.Take: the scale and the silence. Leave: the hostility.
Death StrandingYou build infrastructure for strangers. Other players' roads and ladders appear in your world, and a like counts for more than money.Take: infrastructure as care; asynchronous shared structures.
Oxygen Not IncludedPipes, wires and gravity-fed liquids, with overlay views for each network.Take: one overlay per network; gravity as a design rule.
FrostpunkHeat as a network radiating from a generator; a society under pressure.Take: one network that makes people's faces change.
INFRAYou play an engineer documenting decaying infrastructure in a neglected city.Take: inspection and documentation as play. Tagging is that.
ScreepsAn MMO where your units are your own code, running on a shared server.Take: bots as processes on an API. Leave: code as the only way in.
Dungeon KeeperA hand that picks up and drops, with minions doing the actual work.Take: the hand plus crew split.
Into the BreachEvery enemy telegraphs its next move.Take: forecasts turn reaction into planning.
Citizen SleeperPrecarious living on a station owned by someone else's economy.Take: residents with names and small stakes.
Rain WorldAn ecosystem that goes about its business without caring about you.Take: the Builders' indifference as atmosphere.
Universal PaperclipsA runaway optimizer, played from the inside.Take: the Foreman side's arc.

P. 50 Letters

Open questions for you

The decisions I couldn't make for you. Each has my lean; mark them and I'll pick up from your answers.

Whose side is the player on?

A Tender who looks after residents, or a free agent who works for whoever pays: residents, Builders, or both.

Lean: Tender. A clear side gives the bargaining stakes.

Do residents have faces?

Named people with small stories, like Citizen Sleeper, or a count per ward with a mood.

Lean: counts, plus a few named regulars per ward who speak in the log.

Is there a fail state?

Game over, or a city that never ends while wards go dark and people move away.

Lean: no game over. Population drifts toward wherever you keep the lights on.

Real time or shifts?

Continuous time with pause, or discrete shifts (night, dawn, day) with a forecast between them.

Lean: real time with pause, structured into shifts. The Janitor sweep at shift change is a natural clock.

Is there a master plan?

Does the growth secretly serve a purpose the game reveals, or is it just many mandates adding up?

Lean: many mandates, no master. The first work order at the bottom is the only mystery, and it should be mundane.

How big is "building" for the player?

Pods and patches hung off other structure, or full district-scale construction.

Lean: pods in Lineman, everything in Garden and Foreman. The placement work serves all three.
