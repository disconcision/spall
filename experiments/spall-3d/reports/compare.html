The differences that do come with 3D are the angle-dependent ones. Thin lines alias as you turn. Roof props are painted flat. Trees and the crane turn with the camera. Top faces show diamond-shaped pixels from steep angles.

Thin diagonal lines

The art is drawn as one-pixel lines for one projection. Off that projection, each screen pixel samples one point of the art. A one-pixel rail foreshortened to 0.7 pixels gets hit by some screen pixels and missed by others, and which ones change as the angle moves. That's the crawl. Plain point sampling can't avoid it. The options:

  • Thin lines (built, press K). When a pixel misses but its footprint covers an opaque art pixel, it takes that pixel, so lines never break up. Lines get slightly bolder at steep angles. The crawl gets smaller but doesn't go away. Compare "New 3D" with "New + thin lines" on the turned views below. Cheap; could be on by default.
  • Snap the angle. Rotate in steps (15°, or Kody King's 9°) and ease between them. The crawl only happens during the tween, and every resting view is one of a few known states. You lose truly free angles. The prototype already eases home to iso on release; stepped orbit is a small change.
  • Supersample while moving. Render at 2–4× and average down while the camera turns, then go crisp when it stops. The crawl becomes a soft blur in motion. It costs 4–16× the fill, and the pixel look softens for a moment each turn.
  • Thin features as real lines. Model rails, posts, cage edges and truss members as GPU lines at least one pixel wide. A rasterised line is always unbroken and steady as it turns. This means restating each type's line art as geometry: moderate work, but the most robust option, and these are exactly the structures cables hang from.
  • Rotation-stable texels. The 2026 texel-splatting work fixes texels in world space so they don't swim. It's research-grade, aimed at perspective cameras, and the costliest to adopt.

Revised after trying it: thin lines (K) isn't the answer. It thickens every line that crosses a foreshortened plane, including the ones that were fine, and still crawls. It stays off by default. To compare it at a fixed odd angle, press O to turn snap off first. Otherwise the view eases back to iso on release.

Real lines: what becomes one, and what it costs

The cut outlines are the test case. The rule I'd use: a feature becomes a line when it is a line in the world, a thing you'd model as a rod or an edge. That covers rails and posts, cage and frame edges, truss members, masts and lamp posts, cut outlines, and the cables themselves. Pattern on a surface stays art: windows, mullions, brick, poché hatching. Surface pattern aliases too, but it moves with its surface and doesn't break into dashes the way a lone rail does.

Maintenance: each such type gets a short line list (rail height, post spacing, which edges) beside its sprite. The 3D view strips those pixels from the art and draws the lines, as it now does for the cut outline. The risk is two descriptions of one rail drifting apart. The fix is to have the 2D sprite draw its rails from the same list, projected the iso way, so there's one source. That's about eight types. It's a contained, one-time cost, not an ongoing one.

Where to draw them: the cut outlines go through the 2D cable overlay, which is fine for a few hundred segments. Rails across the city are thousands, and they need lighting. Those belong on the GPU, as one-pixel-wide strips in the scene pass. That is the same renderer GPU cables would use. So "real lines" and "GPU cables" are one feature: a line renderer.

Rotation-stable texels

Today each screen pixel asks the art which texel lies under its centre. When a plane turns away, several texels crowd into one pixel and some are never asked, so a one-pixel line drops out in a pattern that changes every frame. Splatting runs the other way: every opaque texel is a small point in the world, projected and rounded to a screen pixel. Nothing drops out, because every texel lands somewhere, and a line of texels stays a connected staircase at any angle. As you turn, texels hop one pixel at a time, which reads like a hand-animated sprite turning rather than like crawl. The costs: it replaces the core of the renderer (points instead of textured planes); magnified faces, such as tops seen from above, need bigger splats or they open holes; and texels tied at the same depth need a stable order. It is general, though: no per-type line lists. It's worth a small isolated test before choosing between it and per-type lines.

Smooth while moving

During a drag, render at two or three times the resolution and average down. Lines become soft grey-ish strokes that slide smoothly instead of crawling. When you let go, the view snaps back to crisp pixels. It's cheap to try and generic, but every turn briefly loses the pixel look, which I think undercuts the point.

Performance

The stalls. The construction drones finish a job about once a second. Each world change made the 3D view rebuild its light volumes on the CPU in one go. That was about 95 ms for the volumes the 2D game also uses, plus about 250 ms for the hard-sun volume, which only the 3D view fills in for every voxel. Now the hard sun is marched on the GPU, and the rest is spread across frames at no more than 4 ms each. The old light stays on screen until the new light is ready.

The steady rate. Every frame, the 2D cable code redraws every cable, pixel by pixel, on the CPU: about 40,000 single-pixel fills. To depth-test them, it waits for the GPU to finish and reads back the depth buffer. The GPU's own work, including the new haze and bloom, is a few milliseconds. The fix is to draw cables on the GPU as strips with the same palettes and ties. That removes both the fills and the wait. It's the largest remaining job, and it's what will make rotation smooth. It isn't essential to 3D; it's a port.
