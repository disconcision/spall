Spall 3D, texel splats

An experiment in the 3D view. Normally each screen pixel looks up the art under it, and that's what makes thin lines crawl. Here every art pixel is sent to the screen instead. It uses the same sprites as the rest of the game; there's no new art and nothing to describe per building type.

In the 3D view, U cycles planes → planes + points → points only. Hold Z to see plain planes while you hold it. O turns snap off so you can hold an odd angle.

Turn it and compare

The same 20 frames of a turn, rendered four ways at one frozen instant, so only the rendering differs. Pick a mode, then compare it with plain Planes (today's renderer) in one of three ways. Hold flips to Planes while you press it; you can also hold Space. Side by side shows both. Differences greys out the picture and marks every pixel that differs from Planes in magenta. The changes are mostly single pixels on rails and edges, so zoom in. Step through the angles with ← → or the slider, and press P to play.

How it works

Every art pixel already has a fixed place in the world: the spot on its plane that it covers in the iso view. The two methods differ in which way they go between that place and the screen.

  • Rotation stability. Nothing is re-derived from the screen, so art pixels move as whole units as you turn. They hop one pixel at a time instead of blinking in and out. The stability comes from the art's grid being fixed to the world.
  • Gaps. When a plane is seen more face-on than in the iso view, for example roofs from above or facades from low down, its art pixels are more than one screen pixel apart. One-pixel points leave holes there. The paper widens each splat to cover them. That's "points only", and it's why lines get chunkier in that mode, much like thin lines (K).
  • The hybrid. "Planes + points" draws the planes as before and then lays a one-pixel point for every art pixel just in front of its plane. The planes fill any holes, and the points put back pixels the planes skipped.

How this relates to the research

The technique comes from Dylan Ebert's Texel Splatting (March 2026). It is written for 3D models under a perspective camera. A model has no pixel grid of its own, so the paper makes one. It renders the scene into a cubemap from a fixed point (the "probe") and shades each cubemap texel once. Then it draws every texel on screen as a small quad in the world, widened to cover gaps. Texels are indexed by their direction from the probe, which makes them independent of where the camera faces. The probe also snaps to a world grid as the camera moves, with a dithered crossfade when it jumps. The limitation the paper names is disocclusion: surfaces the probe can't see are filled from a camera-centred probe, and those areas shimmer again.

Spall has the grid already: it's the sprites. The "probe" is the iso camera, orthographic and infinitely far away. So we take only the second half of the paper, the splatting. That means:

  • Same assets. The points come straight from the existing sprite atlas: no new art, no per-type data.
  • No probe hops. The probe never moves, so turning and panning are stable with nothing to crossfade.
  • No disocclusion. Every plane carries its own art, back faces included (mirrored), so nothing has to be borrowed from the camera's view.

What I see

ModeTurningCost
PlanesThe baseline. Rail posts and the yellow top rail break up and re-form as you turn, worst at low tilt.2 ms
Thin lines (K)Fewer breaks, but posts merge into a dark band and everything thickens.2 ms

Too small to notice at 1×; use Differences at 3–4× to see where.+10 ms*
Points onlyChanges 10–37% of pixels: a visibly different image. The most interesting. At low tilt, posts land as evenly spaced single columns and the rail reads as if drawn at that angle. Where points widen to two pixels (most turned views at 35°), everything gets chunkier.+9 ms*

* Headless Chrome on a software GPU, so these times are pessimistic. This view has 41,000 planes, and each runs 448 vertex slots (one per pixel of its sprite) whether or not they land. Sizing each plane's slots to its sprite and skipping planes that face away would cut most of that. The home view is pixel-identical in all modes but points-only at 35°.

My read: the hybrid is a safe default with no downside except cost. Points only is the more promising look, but it needs a better gap rule than "cover the footprint". Holes only matter on solid surfaces, so the next step is points sized per surface: one pixel along a rail, just enough to close gaps on a roof. If that works, it would replace both thin lines and the per-type real lines for anything drawn in the sprites.
